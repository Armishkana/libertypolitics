    Main quest: Build your voting bloc

    Seven stages. Each one is a mission of its own, and each is worth more than the last.

    0 of 7 done

    • 1Pick your ground5 minutes50 XP

      One House district, or your state for a Senate seat. Smaller is stronger.

      Only for: US citizen, Green card.

      Why. In 2024, 87 in every 100 House races were in effect decided in the primary, by 7 in every 100 eligible voters. Source: Unite America, 8 October 2024 (a group that campaigns to change primaries)

      1. Find your House district on house.gov.
      2. Look up who holds the seat, and their letter here.
      3. Choose: that one district, or the whole state if the seat you care about is in the Senate.

      What to send as proof. The district or state you picked, and who holds the seat.

      • Find your district on house.gov
      • The grades in your state
    • 2Find your first tenEvery week200 XP

      Ten who mean it are worth more than a hundred who clicked a button.

      Only for: US citizen, Green card.

      Why. About one eligible voter in five took part in the 2022 primaries. In a race that small, a few hundred people who all turn up are a large share of the room. Source: Bipartisan Policy Center report on 2022 primary turnout, as summarised by Unite America

      1. Start with people you already talk politics with: your church, your range, the other parents, work.
      2. Post one line in your state's discussion: where you live, and that you're starting a bloc.
      3. Keep going until ten people have said yes.

      What to send as proof. How many said yes, and where you found them.

      Do not post anyone's full name, address or phone number.

      • Open your state's discussion
    • 3Agree on three thingsAn hour250 XP

      A group that wants everything gets nothing. Pick the three you will not bend on.

      Only for: US citizen, Green card.

      Why. Since 1986, Americans for Tax Reform has asked candidates to sign a written pledge to oppose tax increases, and publishes who has signed. Source: Americans for Tax Reform, About the pledge

      1. Read the twelve measures I grade Congress on. You don't have to invent the list.
      2. Meet, and pick the three things your group votes on.
      3. Write them on one page. Everyone who joins agrees to vote on those three.

      What to send as proof. Your three things, in your own words.

      • The twelve measures and every vote behind them
    • 4Make yourselves countableEvery week300 XP

      A politician can't fear a mood. Know your number.

      Only for: US citizen, Green card.

      Why. Meeting, agreeing on what you vote on, visiting an office, knocking on doors and posting online are free to do. An unpaid volunteer is not making a contribution. Source: FEC, Volunteer activity

      1. Keep a list: name, street, registered or not.
      2. Get every name registered and checked. Some state deadlines fall a month before the election.
      3. Count the registered voters on your list. That is your number.

      What to send as proof. Your number: how many registered voters are on your list.

      Send the number only. Never send the list.

      • Your state's deadlines, and how to register there
      • Register, or check that you are, at vote.gov
      • Deadlines by state on usa.gov
    • 5Ask on the recordAn hour350 XP

      Go to the district office as a group. Three questions. A yes or a no to each.

      Only for: US citizen, Green card.

      Why. Asked what moves a member of Congress who has not made up their mind, 94 in 100 congressional staff said a visit in person from people who live in the district. For a form email it was 56. Source: Congressional Management Foundation, Citizen-Centric Advocacy, 2017, figure 1

      1. Go in person, together, with the member's page from this site in your hand.
      2. Ask one question for each of your three things. Ask for a yes or a no.
      3. Write down the answer, the date and who gave it. Ask every challenger the same three.
      4. Post the answers in that politician's discussion here, with where they can be checked.

      What to send as proof. The link to your post here, or to the video or letter that holds the answers.

      • Find your senator or representative
      • Find your race and everyone running in it
    • 6Turn out your bloc, then tell themEvery week400 XP

      The primary first. Then one short letter to the winner and to the loser.

      Only for: US citizen, Green card.

      Why. A study of the Tea Party years found that in districts where local groups pressed candidates in primaries, the way the district was represented moved in their direction. Source: Blum and Cowburn, British Journal of Political Science, 2023

      1. See that every voter on your list votes. The primary comes first: it's where most seats are decided.
      2. After the election, write one short letter to the winner and one to the loser.
      3. Say how many of you there are, and the three things you voted on.

      What to send as proof. How many on your list voted, and who got your letter.

      Never photograph a marked ballot. That is against the law in some states.

      • How to vote in your state
    • 7Keep the score between electionsEvery week500 XP

      A promise is tested at the next vote. Thank them or call them out, in public.

      Only for: US citizen, Green card.

      Why. The Armenian National Committee of America grades every sitting senator and representative on its issues and publishes the report cards before each election. Source: ANCA report card

      1. Watch your member's grade here. It moves as Congress votes.
      2. When they keep their word, thank them in public. When they break it, say so in public.
      3. Bring the page to your next meeting with the office.

      What to send as proof. The link to what you said in public.

      You can do this one again: one accepted report a week.

      The reading behind the board

      Why a small group wins
      • Most seats are settled before November. In 2024, 87 in every 100 House races were in effect decided in the primary, by 7 in every 100 eligible voters.Source: Unite America, 8 October 2024 (a group that campaigns to change primaries)
      • Almost nobody votes in a primary. About one eligible voter in five took part in the 2022 primaries. In a race that small, a few hundred people who all turn up are a large share of the room.Source: Bipartisan Policy Center report on 2022 primary turnout, as summarised by Unite America
      • Offices listen to voters who show up. Asked what moves a member of Congress who has not made up their mind, 94 in 100 congressional staff said a visit in person from people who live in the district. For a form email it was 56.Source: Congressional Management Foundation, Citizen-Centric Advocacy, 2017, figure 1

      The 2026 primaries are over, so November 3 is your bloc's first count. The primary in 2028 is where it can decide a seat. Groups that start now are the ones a candidate has to visit then.

      It has been done before

      None of this is new. The groups that politicians fear most are rarely the largest. They are the ones that keep a score and vote on it.

      • A report card. The Armenian National Committee of America grades every sitting senator and representative on its issues and publishes the report cards before each election. Congress has an Armenian caucus led by two Republicans and two Democrats.Source: ANCA report card, Congressional Armenian Caucus
      • A written pledge. Since 1986, Americans for Tax Reform has asked candidates to sign a written pledge to oppose tax increases, and publishes who has signed.Source: Americans for Tax Reform, About the pledge
      • Local groups in primaries. A study of the Tea Party years found that in districts where local groups pressed candidates in primaries, the way the district was represented moved in their direction.Source: Blum and Cowburn, British Journal of Political Science, 2023
      The law, in five lines

      For federal races. This is my reading of the Federal Election Commission's own pages, linked under each line. Your state has its own rules for state and local races.

      • Meeting and talking need no permission. Meeting, agreeing on what you vote on, visiting an office, knocking on doors and posting online are free to do. An unpaid volunteer is not making a contribution.Source: FEC, Volunteer activity
      • Money changes things at $1,000. A group whose main purpose is to influence federal elections has to register with the Commission within ten days of raising or spending more than $1,000 in a calendar year.Source: FEC, Registering a nonconnected committee
      • What one person may give. In 2025 and 2026 an individual may give a candidate up to $3,500 for each election. The primary and the general election count separately.Source: FEC, Contribution limits
      • Green card holders. A lawful permanent resident may give money and volunteer like a citizen, but may not vote in a federal election.Source: FEC, Foreign nationals, usa.gov, Who can and cannot vote
      • Everyone else who is not a citizen. May volunteer without pay and may speak about any issue. May not give or spend money on an election, and may not take part in a group's decisions about elections.Source: FEC, Foreign nationals
      What this site does, and what it does not

      I publish the record: every vote I count, every grade, and the candidates in every race side by side. That's the part a group can't easily build for itself.

      I do not endorse or oppose any candidate. I do not take money for any bloc, and I do not run one or decide anything for one. Yours is yours.
